You've highlighted the typical dangers of patterns - traders tend to see patterns too frequently and see what they want to see rather than what is actually there.

This is one of the reasons understanding what is actually happening in patterns like flags in terms of the price action helps to assess the 'quality' of the pattern and the likelihood of it working out.

The most common mistake I see people make (not on here - just generally) is with regards H&S patterns, people see them in all shapes and sizes without regard of the location relative to other TF's and to the market generally - let alone an understanding on what is actually occurring in an H&S pattern from a true price action perspective.

You've also highlighted the nuances off going from a relatively quiet session (Asia) to Frankfurt / London open - it's easy to see Asian low liquidity and thus tight consolidation as a flag or other pattern, taking care when you are approaching such a big jump in volume is always prudent IMO.

With regards flags specifically, another thing I would generally comment on is to remember what a flag is:

1. It's a continuation of a strong momentum move - it's a brief pause while price clears demand (for a short setup) and then moves on it's way. Thus it is logical that if the pole forms say over 2 hours and then price consolidates in the flag for 12 hours then the 'chances' of the flag working have somewhat decreased - because you're looking for that same momentum (which is a strong supply / demand imbalance) being present and price moving in the same vein it did when forming the pole.

2. The chances of a flag also working out if it retraces the whole pole distance are also decreased, why, because as a I said above a flag is a momentum continuation move so if price is retracing the whole move what does that tell us about the strength of the move that caused the pole - that supply / demand imbalance has been completely retraced.

Of course that latter could turn out to be compression back into the source of the pole for example, but then that's something different and not a flag.

I find when first understanding patterns if people (a) remember to think about what the pattern represents should be happening (b) understand the price action occurring within the pattern (c) remember to check the location and surrounding TF's and (d) check the market generally it helps to keep them out of trouble and focussing on identifying the more straight forward and obvious setups which helps to build confidence before moving onto more advanced concepts.

Quickly scanned the charts to find two examples of more obvious straightforward flags. Notice how price moved after it forms the flag lows/highs i.e. hits supply for the short and demand for the long (you should always be able to identify a clear supply / demand zone where price will start to move with high momentum and breakout of the flag).

The advantage of having the above knowledge is you know how price should move and where it should move from and thus can get into a trade at the biggest discount and easily cut the trade when price isn't doing what it should. Catching moves like this when you're in at the source of the flag provide some very big risk/reward opportunities.

Josh wrote: With regards flags specifically, another thing I would generally comment on is to remember what a flag is

A flag is either one of two things; profit taking or consumption. Why do you see constantly flags at key levels before they break? At key levels there are a crap load of orders sitting there (lovely liquidity) in which the market makers take advantage of to re-accumulate. If they were net long for example and price gets to a level of sell orders, they use it as an opportunity to add to their positions - consuming them. When they get to their level of taking profits they will release their longs as sales into the market which creates a beflag pattern. It consolidates but does not drop too much because they release them slowly to keep it within a price range to maintain profit.
